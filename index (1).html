What the Research Tells Us

The real science behind what Mia discovers in the forest

🧠

Nature Calms the Nervous System

Time outdoors lowers cortisol (the stress hormone), slows heart rate, and shifts children from high alert to calm readiness — exactly what happens to Mia when she sits under the Mother Tree. Even 20 minutes outside makes a measurable difference.

Li et al., International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health
📚

Attention and Focus Improve

Natural environments restore the directed attention children need for reading, math, and learning. Children with ADHD show especially strong focus improvements after time in green settings — no medication required.

Faber Taylor & Kuo, American Journal of Public Health
😊

Mood and Emotional Regulation

Children who spend regular time in nature show lower anxiety, greater resilience, and higher self-esteem. Like Mia on her hard day — sitting under a tree doesn't fix the problem, but it helps the body find its way back to calm.

Chawla, L., Journal of Planning Literature
🌐

The Wood Wide Web Is Real

Everything in the book about the mycorrhizal network is real science. Trees genuinely share nutrients and communicate through underground fungal threads. This research was pioneered by forest ecologist Dr. Suzanne Simard — the real Ms. Rowan.

Simard, S. (2021). Finding the Mother Tree. Knopf.

⚠ Signs of Nature Disconnection

  • Persistent restlessness indoors
  • Difficulty winding down or sleeping
  • Trouble focusing on tasks at home
  • Increased irritability without clear cause
  • Low interest in unstructured play
  • Heightened dependence on screens

✓ Signs Nature Is Helping

  • Noticeably calmer after outdoor time
  • More creative and imaginative play
  • Better sleep on active outdoor days
  • Increased curiosity and observation
  • Improved mood within 10–15 minutes outside
  • More willing to try new things

The Two Breathing Exercises

Simple tools your child can use anytime they feel overwhelmed — from the book

🌳 Tree Breathing

Pages 41–42 in the book

Stand tall and imagine you are a tree. Uses movement and slow breathing together to calm the nervous system quickly. Works great at bedtime or before anything stressful.

  • 1ROOT — Press feet into the ground. Feel strong and solid. Hold 3 seconds.
  • 2GROW — Breathe IN for 4 counts, stretching arms up like branches.
  • 3SWAY — Hold the breath, gently sway arms like a breeze. 2 counts.
  • 4SETTLE — Breathe OUT for 6 counts, slowly lower arms down.

Repeat 3 times. Try it alongside your child — Tree Breathing works for adults too!

🍃 Leaf Breathing

Pages 44–45 in the book

Trace the edge of a leaf slowly with your finger, breathing in sync with the movement. Quiet, simple, and can be done anywhere — even at a school desk or in the car.

  • 1Find a real leaf — or use the leaf printed on page 45.
  • 2Trace the edge slowly with one finger.
  • 3Breathe IN as your finger goes up one side.
  • 4Breathe OUT as your finger comes down the other side.
  • 5Repeat 5 times. Notice how you feel.

Fun fact from the book: leaves breathe through tiny holes called stomata. Your child and the tree are breathing together!

Practical Tips for Home

No forest required — these work anywhere

📖 Read Together First

Before your child does any activity page, read the story pages aloud together. Ask "Did you know trees could really do that?" Let the wonder land before moving to the activity.

🌿 Take It Outside

The Leaf Breathing page works best outdoors. Bring the book on a walk or to a park — even a city street with one tree works. Finding a real leaf makes the exercise feel special.

🌳 Practice Breathing Together

Both exercises work for adults too. Doing them alongside your child models that calming your nervous system is for everyone — not just overwhelmed kids.

🌱 Start Something Growing

Even one pot on a windowsill counts. Let your child plant a seed and water it daily — just like Mia did when she came back to the city. Watching something grow builds the same sense of care the book nurtures.

🐛 Follow Their Curiosity

If your child stops to look at a bug, a mushroom, or a bird — stop with them. Resisting the urge to hurry past is one of the most powerful nature-connection habits you can build together.

🏅 Display the Certificate

The Forest Whisperer Certificate at the back is meant to be cut out and kept. Displaying it somewhere visible signals to your child that this identity matters to you too.

Conversation Starters

For the car, dinner table, or bedtime — no nature required

If you were a tree, what kind would you be and why?
Who is the "Mother Tree" in our family — someone who shares and takes care of everyone?
Where do you feel most calm? What does that place look like?
Do you think trees really have feelings? Why or why not?
What's one thing you'd protect if you could protect anything in nature?
If the Mother Tree could talk to you, what do you think she'd say?
When you feel worried or stuck, what helps your body calm down?
What's one small thing we could do to help nature near where we live?
Leaves breathe through tiny holes called stomata — what else do you think trees do that scientists haven't discovered yet?
Mia planted a garden when she got home. If you could plant anything, what would it be?

Forest Words to Know

Every vocabulary word from the book — with pronunciations and plain-English definitions

Fungi
Say it: FUN-guy

Tiny living things that grow in soil — like mushrooms! In the forest, fungi grow as thread-like networks between tree roots, helping trees communicate and share food underground.

Mycelium
Say it: my-SEE-lee-um

The thread-like roots that fungi use to connect trees underground. Mycelium is what makes the Wood Wide Web possible — it carries water, nutrients, and warning signals between trees.

Nervous System
No special pronunciation!

The part of your body that tells you if you feel safe, scared, or calm. Nature helps calm the nervous system — which is why the breathing exercises in the book are so effective.

Nutrients
Say it: NEW-tree-ents

The good stuff in food that helps living things grow strong. Trees share nutrients through the Wood Wide Web — especially Mother Trees sharing with baby trees that can't reach enough sunlight.

Wood Wide Web
No special pronunciation!

The nickname scientists gave to the underground network of fungi and roots connecting trees in a forest. Like the internet — but for trees. First described by Dr. Suzanne Simard.

Stomata
Say it: stoh-MAH-tah

Tiny holes in leaves that let plants breathe — taking in carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen. During Leaf Breathing, your child and the leaf are breathing together through stomata!
